Gameplay

Limited power makes strategy matter.

Enter a living historical society from a particular character's seat. You govern within the authority, knowledge, resources, institutions, and distance available to you—and live with what happens after an order leaves your desk.

Gameplay Systems

These systems determine whether intent can become reality.

These are the project's player-facing design directions: organize plans around two to four visible strategic priorities, work through delegation and institutional routines, and judge consequences through dated, sourced reports.

Seat and Orders

Who Are You, and Who Can You Actually Direct?

You choose a position of power: emperor, grand secretary, field commander, garrison leader, or staff adviser—each with different authority, sightlines, resources, and risks. The interface presents the current plan as two to four visible strategic priorities.

What You Navigate

  1. Choose a historical seat with real authority and blind spots, then define two to four current strategic priorities.
  2. Turn intent into direct orders, formal requests, private influence, or work delegated through officials and existing institutions.
  3. Re-plan around estimated delivery, expected reporting, and actual acknowledgement.

Local Friction

Why Does the Local World Push Back?

Counties, gentry, salt merchants, soldiers, refugees, roads, and granaries decide whether an arrived order becomes action, delay, bargaining, or a new crisis.

What You Navigate

  1. Read who actually controls a place, and who can provide cooperation.
  2. Divide scarce resources between relief, requisition, route repair, route security, credit, and tax pressure.
  3. Watch morale, refugees, food prices, rumor, and legitimacy pull on each other.

War Pressure

Why Is War Harder Than Pushing a Front?

Armies can defend, contest crossings, and pressure local society, but they also consume grain and pay, damage order, and create debt. Battles emerge from routes, supply, commanders, morale, and local tolerance.

What You Navigate

  1. Choose what to defend, rescue, abandon, and whether your orders can arrive in time.
  2. Trade off battle risk, supply pressure, local tolerance, and expanding military power.
  3. Deal with occupation, defeat, discipline, and front-line autonomy.

Consequences

How Does the Situation Continue—and How Do You Change It?

Recognition, truces, loans, reform, rumor, and historical memory keep working whether or not you intervene. Structural pressures keep an unattended simulation broadly historical; your choices alter what costs the next round can bear.

What You Navigate

  1. Use diplomacy, recognition, and exchange to buy time and create room for military action.
  2. Read causal reports that separate immediate triggers, long pressures, and remaining paths to recovery.
  3. Let earlier choices persist as credibility, grievances, institutional precedent, and material loss.
